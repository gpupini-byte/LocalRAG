The fictional Cedar Ridge Observatory explains lunar phases as changes in the portion of the Moon's sunlit half visible from Earth. The Moon does not make its own visible light; sunlight illuminates half of it at a time. As the Moon travels around Earth, the viewing geometry changes and observers see a sequence of phases. A new moon occurs when the illuminated side mostly faces away from Earth, while a full moon occurs when the visible side is fully illuminated. The observatory's beginner guide cautions readers not to confuse the phases with Earth's shadow, which is involved in a lunar eclipse rather than the ordinary monthly cycle.

The Cedar Ridge guide explains that Earth's seasons are caused mainly by the tilt of Earth's rotational axis as the planet orbits the Sun. When a hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, it receives more direct sunlight and longer daylight hours, producing warmer seasonal conditions. Six months later that hemisphere tilts away, with lower Sun angles and shorter days. The changing Earth-Sun distance is not the primary cause of the seasons. The guide uses a tilted lamp and globe demonstration to show why the two hemispheres experience opposite seasons at the same time. The example is simplified, but it helps distinguish axial tilt from orbital distance.

For beginner telescope use, the observatory recommends first choosing a stable tripod and observing a bright, easy target such as the Moon. The telescope should be allowed to adjust to outdoor temperature, and the observer should begin with a low-power eyepiece that provides a wider field of view. A finder scope helps aim the main instrument. The guide advises keeping a short observing log with the date, target, eyepiece, and sky conditions. It warns against pointing any telescope at the Sun without a certified full-aperture solar filter, because concentrated sunlight can cause permanent eye injury.
