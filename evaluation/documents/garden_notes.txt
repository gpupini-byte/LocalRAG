The fictional North Window Garden uses compost to improve heavy soil. The gardeners add finished compost to the top layer before spring planting and mix it lightly into the bed. Finished compost is dark and crumbly and no longer contains recognizable food scraps. Compost improves soil structure and helps it retain water, but it is not a complete substitute for checking what a plant needs. The garden team records the date and approximate amount of each amendment so that changes in plant growth can be compared from season to season. These notes describe a made-up community garden for a reproducible software benchmark.

In the North Window Garden, watering is done slowly near the base of plants in the early morning. The schedule is adjusted after rainfall rather than following a rigid daily timer. Gardeners check the soil a short distance below the surface; if it is still moist, they postpone watering. A layer of mulch reduces evaporation and helps keep soil temperature steadier. Newly planted seedlings receive closer attention than established plants. The notes recommend observing the bed and recording dry periods, because shade, wind, and container size can change how quickly soil loses moisture.

The garden supports pollinators by planting several flowering species with different bloom times. A small patch of native flowers is left undisturbed, and pesticide use is avoided while flowers are open. The team keeps a shallow dish with stones so insects can reach water without landing in deep water. Gardeners observe which flowers attract bees and butterflies and add those observations to the seasonal notebook. The notes do not claim that one plant will attract every pollinator; local climate and species affect the result. The shared goal is to provide food and shelter over more than one part of the growing season.
